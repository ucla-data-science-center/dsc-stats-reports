Research Enabled

Pathway: one-on-one research data support at UCLA, and the capacity behind it

Finding and scope

DSC’s staff time fell from 5.7 FTE in 2022-23 to 4.0 in 2025-26, and student help fell by half in 2025, while consultation records became incomplete. Researchers across UCLA bring data problems to DSC, and some of those consultations become multi-year collaborations. DSC recorded about 1,850 scheduled consultations from 2017 to September 2026. Recorded counts rise and fall with the staff and students available to do the work and with the system used to record it, so they show the work delivered, not the demand for it.

  • Scheduling systems changed three times: Calendly to mid-2024, no scheduling system until May 2025, then LibCal. Consultations continued in the gap, but most went unrecorded.

Three collaborations

These three were chosen to show different kinds of documented support, not typical outcomes: data preparation and computing, a dataset a lab maintains itself, and 3D capture. For scale and breadth, see the consultation series above, the instruction pathway, and the shared investments page.

Police helicopter surveillance (Carceral Ecologies)

The Carceral Ecologies lab, directed by Nicholas Shapiro in UCLA’s Institute for Society and Genetics, studies the health and mental-health effects of law-enforcement helicopter surveillance across Los Angeles County from 2017 through 2022 (lab project page). Its public findings include that LAPD helicopters fly lowest over largely Black and Hispanic neighborhoods, with a median of about 550 feet over Watts at night (CalMatters, July 2024).

When COVID closed the prisons where the lab had done environmental field sampling, Shapiro moved its work to data analysis, and the flight-trajectory dataset soon outgrew what the lab could host itself. In his words, DSC and DataSquad supported the lab “through technical assistance, helping one of my research students (who became staff) grow her R skills, and in hosting the R server that makes this research possible.”

A student researcher on the team first learned R and GIS from DSC staff in a 2019 African American Studies course taught with DSC. In 2022 she came back with the team’s flight-tracking data, twelve monthly files of about a million rows each, and met DSC staff and DataSquad consultants more than 30 times that year (her 2023 interview). Together they converted the raw flight records into analyzable tables, identified individual flights, filtered out impossible altitude readings, and joined millions of positions to Los Angeles neighborhoods (her analysis code). DSC’s GIS librarian advised on the spatial analysis, recommending a density-based clustering method for classifying the flight data and helping fix geometry problems in the spatial files. When the processing outgrew her laptop that fall, DSC moved the data onto its deep learning machine and gave her access to its hosted R server.

As the lab acquired more data, DSC helped it scale. In March 2023 DSC staff met with Shapiro’s team, set up the lab’s new statistician on the deep learning machine, and loaded five years of additional data the lab had purchased. Over the following months DSC staff and DataSquad students worked with the statistician to process the dataset in batches and speed up elevation lookups about tenfold by switching data sources (shared code). One measured piece of that work cut the time to load the data from about 318 minutes to about 2, about 159 times as fast (DataSquad project page). DSC staff on the collaboration included Tim Dennis, Leigh Phan, Zhiyuan Yao, and Kristian Allen.

In Shapiro’s account, the lab’s ability to keep analyzing its growing flight data has depended on that technical help and on the R server DSC hosts.

“This work wouldn’t have happened without your team!”

Nicholas Shapiro, director of Carceral Ecologies, November 2024 (quoted with permission)

The research and its findings belong to the lab; Shapiro received a 2024 UCLA Public Impact Research Award for it (Institute for Society and Genetics, August 2024).

Deaths in custody (BioCritical Studies Lab)

In January 2021, Terence Keel’s BioCritical Studies Lab, in UCLA’s Institute for Society and Genetics, set out to build a single dataset of deaths in custody across the United States from 2000 to 2020. Those deaths are reported by several crowd-based sources, including The Guardian, Fatal Encounters, and Mapping Police Violence, each with its own variables and standards, and the dataset has to be revised as the sources change.

DataSquad students Julia Wood and Ethan Allavarpu merged the sources, reconciled discrepancies between them, and identified deaths that some sources had missed. They then built maps and dashboards, including Los Angeles maps, and ran statistical comparisons of death rates that, in the words of the lab’s assistant director, were “instrumental in informing us which locations we should prioritize” (UCLA Library, May 2022). DSC staff later helped the lab organize and document its data. In 2024 DataSquad student Lawrence Lee developed two codebooks that let the lab integrate and clean new data from any source, and taught the lab to use them so it could update the dataset on its own.

Keeping the national dataset usable through new sources and revisions has depended on recurring DataSquad work and on tools the lab learned to use itself.

“There is a lack of comprehensive and reliable data on death in custody in the United States, and the work the DataSquad has done has helped us address this need and has allowed us to maintain this dataset over time and through revisions.”

Grace Sosa, assistant director, BioCritical Studies Lab, August 2024 (quoted with permission)

In the same letter, Sosa writes that the lab has used the dataset to complete projects funded by the National Institutes of Health, the National Science Foundation, and the Robert Wood Johnson Foundation. The research is the lab’s; Keel, like Shapiro, received a 2024 UCLA Public Impact Research Award (Institute for Society and Genetics, August 2024).

3D capture for the humanities (Refugee Material Culture Initiative)

Classics professor Kelly Nguyen’s Refugee Material Culture Initiative, a collaboration with the Vietnamese Heritage Museum in Garden Grove, digitally preserves art and artifacts made and used by refugees (Cotsen Institute of Archaeology). Doug Daniels, DSC’s emerging technologies librarian, 3D scanned the “Boat of Hope,” a six-meter river sampan that carried nine refugees out of Vietnam in 1984, and prepared the models for the museum’s digital exhibit (the initiative’s page on Doug).

Explore the Boat of Hope and its story in 3D

In Nguyen’s course “Decolonizing Refugee Data from Rome to Vietnam,” students build digital exhibitions from artifacts like these. Nguyen, working with Doug, also organized a digital archaeology day that brought more than 50 Westminster High School students and more than 30 elders from Little Saigon to campus (UCLA Humanities, April 2026; UCLA Newsroom, July 2026).

Doug also spent two weeks in Iceland’s Mosfell Valley running drone surveys for a doctoral student at the Cotsen Institute of Archaeology (UCLA Newsroom, July 2026). He processed data as it arrived during fieldwork and set up a library workstation so the student could keep working after returning; student employee Connor Lim processed the survey data once it came back.

The boat model lives on pointcloud.ucla.edu, a server Doug built in 2022 so that anyone can explore 3D data in an ordinary web browser, as does Iceland turf-architecture survey data; student employee Bianca Badajos helped set up its Potree viewer. Publishing processed data there lets researchers examine it without specialized hardware and lets instructors bring it into class.

The public boat model depends on DSC’s capture, processing, and continued hosting, alongside the initiative’s and the museum’s stewardship of the boat and its story.

The scholarship belongs to Nguyen’s initiative and the Cotsen researchers. Doug was named a 2026 UCLA Librarian of the Year for his work across campus (UCLA Newsroom, July 2026).

NoteThe long view (history, as of 2026)

The archive answered research questions from its early years: data discovery, codebooks, and help with statistical software. The 2018 redesign widened consulting to the whole research life cycle, from data management plans to publication and preservation, and added GIS, 3D capture, machine learning, research software, and reproducible workflows. From about 2023 the work shifted from single appointments toward project support that ran for months. Read the consulting section of the history
